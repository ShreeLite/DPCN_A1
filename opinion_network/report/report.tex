\section{Dataset Documentation}
\label{sec:dataset}

The data cleaning and encoding pipeline below was implemented as part of the network
construction workstream (\texttt{build\_network.py}); this section documents that
process and adds further exploration on top of it.

The raw survey collected responses from 96 students across 60 Likert-scale statements
(Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree) split evenly into four topics of 15 items each:
Technology (\texttt{T}), Education (\texttt{E}), Ethics/Society (\texttt{S}), and
Environment (\texttt{V}). ``No Comments'' was treated as a missing response rather than
a neutral opinion, and respondents leaving more than 50\% of the survey blank were
dropped (9 of 96), leaving 87 usable respondents. Remaining sparse gaps were filled with
each item's class-average response. Responses were then encoded numerically as Strongly
Disagree $=-2$ through Strongly Agree $=+2$, producing the $87 \times 60$ matrix
(\texttt{outputs/encoded\_responses.csv}) that both networks in this report are built
from.

\subsection*{Descriptive Statistics and Data Exploration}

Table~\ref{tab:topic_stats} and Figure~\ref{fig:item_mean_dist} summarize the cleaned
response matrix by topic. Every topic has a positive mean -- the class leans toward
agreement almost everywhere -- but Environment and Ethics/Society are far more
consensual (means 1.39/1.28, std.\ 0.75/0.83, $\sim$88\% Agree or above) than Education
(mean 0.91, std.\ 1.15, the most contested topic). The most contested individual items
are all Education items on assessment/attendance policy (e.g.\ ``class attendance
should be compulsory''), where personal experience plausibly varies more than for
abstract ethics/environment statements; the most consensual items are broad normative
statements (e.g.\ ``companies should be held accountable for environmental impact'')
that Likert surveys rarely see genuine disagreement on.

\paragraph{A note on self-report survey bias.} This skew is consistent with several
well-known self-report biases: \textbf{social desirability} (agreeing with normatively
``good'' positions -- sustainability, ethics -- regardless of true attitude, especially
when responses aren't fully anonymous to peers); \textbf{acquiescence} (a general
tendency to agree, consistent with even Education having a positive mean); and
\textbf{self-selection} (the 87 respondents who completed the survey may be more
agreeable or opinionated than a true random sample; 9 who left it mostly blank were
dropped). Because of this skew, the \emph{relative}, correlation-based structure used
throughout this report (Sections~\ref{sec:pipeline}--\ref{sec:item_network}) is a more
robust signal of real opinion differences than raw agreement levels are.

\section{Pipeline Followed}
\label{sec:pipeline}

This section describes how the raw survey responses were transformed into an opinion
network.

\subsection{Data Cleaning and Encoding}
As detailed in Section~\ref{sec:dataset}: incomplete respondents were dropped (9 of
96), ``No Comments'' was treated as missing, remaining gaps were filled with item
means, and responses were encoded on a $-2$ to $+2$ ordinal scale.

\subsection{Similarity Measure}
Pairwise opinion similarity between respondents was computed using the \textbf{Pearson
correlation coefficient} across their answer vectors, rather than cosine similarity or
Euclidean distance, since correlation captures whether two respondents' opinions rise
and fall \emph{together} and is invariant to a respondent's tendency to answer more or
less extremely overall -- a scale bias rather than a genuine opinion difference.

\subsection{Graph Construction}
The dense $87 \times 87$ similarity matrix was sparsified using \textbf{mutual
$k$-Nearest-Neighbours} with $k=8$: an edge between respondents $i$ and $j$ was
retained only if each was among the other's top-8 most similar peers, avoiding both an
uninformative fully-connected graph and a hand-tuned similarity threshold. Four
topic-specific sub-networks were built the same way, restricted to each topic's 15
items, to allow cross-topic comparison of clustering structure.

\subsection{Community Detection}
Louvain modularity maximization was applied to the overall network to identify opinion
communities, and independently to each topic sub-network for comparison.

\subsection{Null-Model Statistical Validation}
To assess whether the observed community structure is statistically meaningful rather
than an artefact of the graph's size and degree sequence, Louvain modularity was
compared against a null distribution from 500 random graphs generated with the
\textbf{configuration model} (Molloy--Reed): each preserves the real network's exact
degree sequence while rewiring edges uniformly at random, eliminating community
structure but keeping the degree distribution intact. A $z$-score was computed as
$z = (Q_{\text{obs}} - \bar{Q}_{\text{null}})/\sigma_{\text{null}}$, and a one-tailed
empirical $p$-value as the fraction of null runs with modularity $\geq Q_{\text{obs}}$.
This was repeated for $k \in \{4, 8, 12\}$ to test sensitivity to the sparsification
parameter.

\subsection{Item-Opinion Network Construction}
\label{sec:item_network}
A second network was built with the opposite orientation: nodes are the 60 survey
\emph{items} rather than the 87 respondents, and an edge reflects whether two items
were answered similarly across respondents. This is the exact transpose of the
person-network pipeline: Pearson correlation was computed between every pair of items'
87-respondent answer vectors, and the resulting $60\times 60$ matrix was sparsified with
the same mutual $k$-NN procedure ($k=8$) for direct comparability. Louvain communities
were then compared against the survey's own four a-priori topic labels via Adjusted
Rand Index (ARI), to test whether real opinion structure follows the survey's topic
design or cuts across it.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Person-Opinion Network}

The overall opinion network fragments into approximately 25 Louvain communities at a
modularity of 0.578, indicating that class opinion is organized into many small,
fine-grained groups rather than 2--3 broad ideological camps. Cross-topic ARI shows
that \textbf{Education} (0.181) and \textbf{Technology} (0.101) opinions are the
strongest predictors of a respondent's overall community membership, while
\textbf{Ethics/Society} (0.023) and \textbf{Environment} (0.030) are essentially
uncorrelated with it -- ethics and environmental questions attract broad, cross-cutting
agreement or disagreement that doesn't track the same social groupings visible in
tech/education opinions.

The bridge analysis shows the network's cohesion depends on a handful of specific
individuals rather than broad consensus: respondent 81's neighbors span 5 distinct
communities, more than any other node, making this respondent (and the four others in
Table~\ref{tab:bridges}) structurally critical to keeping otherwise separate opinion
groups connected. The opinion archetypes are clearly interpretable: community 0 (9
members) scores well below the class average on both Ethics/Society and Environment --
a coherent ``skeptic'' archetype -- while community 16 (6 members) scores above average
on every topic, a small ``strong-consensus'' cluster.

\subsection{Item-Opinion Network}

The item-opinion network is noticeably less cleanly separated than the person-opinion
network: Louvain finds 9 communities at modularity $\approx 0.43$ (vs.\ 25 communities
at 0.578 for people), three of which are singleton/near-singleton items that nobody's
answer pattern strongly tracks -- survey items are more weakly and diffusely related to
one another than respondents are to each other.

More importantly, item communities do \emph{not} reproduce the survey's four-topic
design (ARI $=0.143$). Instead, the two largest communities each blend two nominal
topics: one groups most Technology and Education items (a ``modernization /
innovation-attitude'' dimension), the other most Ethics/Society and Environment items
(a ``collective responsibility'' dimension) -- a genuinely different axis of variation
than the survey's category labels suggest.

This explains \emph{why} Ethics/Society and Environment barely predict person-level
clustering (ARI $\approx 0.02$--$0.03$) while Education and Technology do (0.18, 0.10):
Ethics/Society and Environment items are exactly the ones with the highest agreement
and lowest spread (Table~\ref{tab:topic_stats}, means 1.28/1.39, smallest std.). When
almost everyone agrees with an item, it carries little information about how
respondents differ, so it cannot drive person-level structure even though it clusters
tightly with other high-consensus items. Technology and Education items have more
spread (std.\ 0.99/1.15) and thus more genuine disagreement to organize people around.

\subsection{Statistical Validation of Community Structure}

The observed modularity of the primary $k{=}8$ network ($Q=0.578$) is \textbf{4.94
standard deviations above the configuration-model null mean}
($\bar{Q}_{\text{null}} = 0.494 \pm 0.017$), with empirical $p<0.002$ (0 of 500 null
runs matched or exceeded it) -- strong evidence the 25 detected communities are not an
artefact of the degree sequence, but statistically real structure.

Sensitivity analysis across $k \in \{4,8,12\}$ (Table~\ref{tab:k_sensitivity}) reveals a
non-monotone pattern: at $k{=}4$ the $z$-score collapses to $0.88$ ($p=0.190$) --
statistically indistinguishable from noise -- while $k{=}8$ and $k{=}12$ are both
highly significant ($z=4.94$, $5.31$). The slightly higher $z$ at $k{=}12$ does not
argue for preferring it: that graph is considerably denser (224 vs.\ 134 edges),
merging fine-grained structure into only 18 broad clusters and reducing modularity to
$0.427$. $k{=}8$ is thus the best-justified trade-off -- statistically significant
structure ($z\approx4.9$, $p<0.002$) at a sparsity that preserves the fine-grained
groupings of interest.

\toprule
$k$ & Edges & Density & $Q_{\text{obs}}$ & $\bar{Q}_{\text{null}} \pm \sigma$ & $z$-score & $p$-value \\
\midrule
4  & 59  & 0.016 & 0.729 & $0.710 \pm 0.023$ & 0.88 & 0.190 \\

\subsection{Overall Synthesis}

Together, the three analyses tell a consistent story about how this class thinks. The
person-opinion network shows the class is not split into a few broad ideological camps
but into many small, genuine opinion groups (25 communities), and the statistical
validation confirms this fragmentation is real: it is 4.94 standard deviations more
structured than chance would produce at the same size and degree sequence, not noise
dressed up as structure. The item-opinion network explains \emph{where that
fragmentation comes from}: it is not spread evenly across the four survey topics, but
concentrated specifically in Technology and Education attitudes, which form their own
correlated ``modernization'' dimension and are the only topics with enough real
disagreement (highest response spread, lowest cross-topic ARI overlap with consensus
topics) to sort people into different camps. Ethics/Society and Environment, by
contrast, form a separate ``collective responsibility'' item dimension that the class
overwhelmingly agrees on (lowest spread, highest agreement), so despite being
internally coherent as a cluster of questions, it carries almost no power to
distinguish one respondent from another. In short: this class agrees broadly on ethics
and environmental responsibility, but is genuinely and measurably divided -- into many
small, statistically validated pockets rather than two polarized camps -- along
attitudes toward technology and educational modernization.
